# 1. Education

## 1.1 Discuss Both Views

**Prompt**: Some people think that the main purpose of education is to make individuals useful to society. Others say education should help people achieve personal ambitions. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

[Para] People have long debated what the main purpose of education should be.
[Pos] While some argue it should serve society by training a skilled workforce, others believe its main aim is to help individuals reach their personal goals. While I see value in both, I lean more towards the second view.

[T] On the one hand, there is a strong case for seeing education as a tool for social development.
[E] Governments spend large amounts on public schooling because they expect graduates to contribute to the economy, science, and social stability.
[Ex] For example, countries such as South Korea and Germany that have focused on science and engineering have built strong economies.
[L] From this view, education is an investment in shared prosperity, and a country that neglects it pays the price later.

[T] On the other hand, I lean more towards the view that education should mainly serve the individual.
[E] When students are encouraged to study subjects they enjoy, they tend to develop deeper skills and stronger motivation than those forced into a fixed track.
[Ex] A student who studies philosophy because they love it is likely to become more creative and thoughtful than one pushed into a job-training course they have no interest in.
[E] In addition, personal growth is valuable in itself, not just as a way to earn money.
[L] The strength of this view is therefore that it treats students as people first and economic units second.

[Conc] In conclusion, while education clearly benefits society, I believe its main goal should be to help individuals develop their potential and live meaningful lives. A society of motivated learners will naturally produce wider benefits, so the two views are not really in conflict.

## 1.2 Problem / Solution

**Prompt**: In many countries, students are losing interest in studying science subjects. What are the causes of this? What solutions can you suggest?

In many countries, fewer students are choosing science subjects. This essay will look at the main reasons and suggest some practical solutions.

[T] One main cause is that science education is often too theoretical and disconnected from real life.
[E] When students spend years memorising formulas without seeing how they are used, motivation drops.
[Ex] In many schools, teaching still relies on rote learning rather than hands-on experiments, which makes the subject feel dry.
[L] As long as science feels like a list of facts to memorise, students will keep drifting away from it.

[T] Another important factor is the influence of career choices.
[E] Many young people see that careers in business and social media look more exciting and pay better than scientific research.
[Ex] Role models from tech start-ups and entertainment dominate the media, while scientists rarely get the same attention.
[L] Given this imbalance, it is not surprising that fewer students see science as a path worth following.

[T] To address this, schools could use more engaging teaching methods, such as project-based learning and lab work.
[E] Governments and the media could also do more to celebrate scientific achievements and show the range of careers open to science graduates.
[Ex] A case in point is hands-on projects such as building a small solar device, which help students see why what they learn matters.
[L] Combining better teaching with stronger public role models would tackle both root causes at once.

[Conc] In summary, falling interest in science comes from boring teaching and unattractive career images. By updating how science is taught and giving scientific careers more public attention, this trend can be reversed.

# 2. Technology & Media

## 2.1 Agree / Disagree

**Prompt**: Some people say that the internet has made people more isolated. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

[Para] Many people argue that the internet, even though it connects billions of users, has actually made people more socially isolated.
[Pos] While I accept that too much internet use can lead to less face-to-face contact, I largely disagree with this claim, because digital communication has greatly improved how people stay connected.

[T] The main reason for my position is that the internet allows people to keep relationships alive across long distances.
[E] Before email and video calls, moving to another country often meant losing daily contact with family and friends, and letters could take weeks to arrive.
[Ex] Today, a grandparent can watch a grandchild's first steps in real time from thousands of kilometres away, or join a family dinner through a video call.
[L] These technologies have strengthened, not weakened, family ties for millions of people around the world.

[T] Admittedly, there is some truth in the view that social media can create a shallow sense of connection while weakening deeper relationships.
[E] Some studies show that heavy social media users sometimes feel lonelier, possibly because polished online profiles replace real interaction.
[E] However, this is a problem of how the technology is used, not a fault of the technology itself. When used carefully, the internet gives people new ways to build communities and find support.
[Ex] Online forums have connected people with rare diseases, niche hobbies, or minority backgrounds who might otherwise feel very alone.
[L] Seen this way, isolation comes from poor digital habits rather than from the internet itself.

[Conc] In conclusion, while heavy or shallow internet use can add to isolation, the technology itself has been a powerful way for people to connect. The answer lies in teaching better digital habits, not in rejecting digital communication.

## 2.2 Discuss Both Views

**Prompt**: Some believe that the media should be free to publish any news without government interference. Others think that media freedom should have limits. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

[Para] How much freedom the media should have is one of the most debated issues in modern democracies. While some support full press freedom, others argue that some limits are needed. This essay will look at both views.

[T] Supporters of full media freedom argue that a free press is essential for keeping governments accountable.
[E] History shows that censorship often serves those in power rather than the public, and citizens cannot make informed choices when information is filtered.
[Ex] Investigative journalism has uncovered corruption, election fraud, and human rights abuses that would otherwise have stayed hidden from public view.
[L] Without this watchdog role, democracy itself becomes much weaker.

[T] On the other hand, those who support some regulation point to the real harm that an unchecked media can cause.
[E] False reporting can destroy innocent people's reputations, and the spread of false information can damage public health and democratic processes.
[Ex] During the recent pandemic, for example, misleading articles about treatments led many people to take dangerous risks with their health, and some social media platforms became major channels for false information.
[L] When there are no limits at all, the freedom of the press can come at the cost of the freedom of those it harms.

[Conc] In my view, media freedom is a basic right that should be strongly protected, but it must also come with responsibility. The best way to achieve this balance is not through government censorship, which is easily abused by those in power, but through independent regulators, clear defamation laws, and industry codes of ethics that can hold journalists accountable without silencing them.

# 3. Globalisation

## 3.1 Discuss Both Views

**Prompt**: Some think globalisation is positive. Others believe it mainly benefits rich countries. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

[Para+Pos] Globalisation has caused a lot of debate about whether it benefits all countries equally or mainly serves wealthy ones. This essay will look at both sides before giving my own view.

[T] Supporters of globalisation argue that it has lifted hundreds of millions of people out of poverty by opening global markets.
[E] Countries like China and Vietnam have grown quickly by joining international trade and bringing in new technologies, which has created jobs and raised living standards.
[Ex] In addition, cultural exchange through travel and media has broadened people's horizons and helped them understand each other better.
[L] From this view, globalisation has been one of the main drivers of human progress over the past few decades.

[T] Critics, however, argue that globalisation has widened the gap between rich and poor countries.
[E] Big multinational companies often use cheap labour in developing countries while sending profits back to their home countries. Local industries, especially small farms and family businesses, may not be able to compete with foreign giants that have huge marketing budgets.
[Ex] On top of that, the spread of one global culture threatens local traditions and languages, and younger generations sometimes lose interest in their own heritage in favour of imported entertainment.
[L] For these critics, the benefits of globalisation are real but unfairly shared between countries and within them.

[Conc] In my view, globalisation is neither fully positive nor fully negative. The answer is not to stop it but to manage it more fairly through better trade agreements, stronger labour protections, and clear policies that protect cultural diversity and small local businesses.

## 3.2 Agree / Disagree

**Prompt**: The spread of multinational companies produces positive effects for everyone. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

[Para] Many people argue that the spread of multinational companies brings benefits to everyone.
[Pos] While I accept that these trends have produced large economic growth, I partly disagree with the idea that the effects are positive for all groups in society.

[T] On the positive side, multinational companies create jobs and bring new technology to host countries.
[E] When a global firm opens a factory or office in a developing country, it often trains local workers in new skills and introduces modern management practices.
[Ex] For consumers around the world, competition between multinationals has led to better products at lower prices, from smartphones to clothing.
[L] These gains are real and have helped millions of people enjoy a higher standard of living.

[T] However, the picture is not entirely positive for everyone.
[E] Small local businesses often cannot compete with the huge marketing budgets of multinationals, which leads to closures and job losses in traditional sectors of the economy.
[E] Workers at the bottom of global supply chains often face poor working conditions and low wages, even when companies make public promises to treat them well.
[Ex] Reports of factories in some Asian countries paying very low wages while producing expensive branded goods sold in rich countries are a clear example of this imbalance.
[L] In other words, the benefits flow more easily to consumers and shareholders than to the workers who actually make the products.

[Conc] In conclusion, while globalisation and the spread of multinationals have brought real benefits, claiming the effects are positive for everyone is too simple. Without clear policies to share the gains fairly and protect vulnerable workers, globalisation risks making inequality worse rather than better.

# 4. Culture & Customs

## 4.1 Agree / Disagree

**Prompt**: It is inevitable that traditional cultures will be lost as technology develops. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

[Para] Some people say that as technology develops, traditional cultures will inevitably disappear.
[Pos] While I accept that technology creates challenges for cultural preservation, I strongly disagree that this loss is unavoidable.

[T] It is true that fast modernisation has put many traditions at risk in recent decades.
[E] As younger generations grow up surrounded by global digital culture, they may show less interest in traditional crafts, ceremonies, and languages.
[Ex] In many rural areas, for example, young people prefer to watch international video content rather than learn songs, recipes, and stories from their grandparents, and some traditional skills are now in real danger of being lost forever.
[L] In this sense, technology clearly does put pressure on traditional cultures, which is why the worry is understandable.

[T] However, the same technology that threatens traditions can also help protect them.
[Ex] Digital archives can record endangered languages before the last speakers pass away. Social media lets communities share their cultural practices with global audiences. YouTube has turned little-known folk musicians into international stars and given them new sources of income.
[E] In addition, Japan shows that modern life and tradition can exist together — a strong tech industry alongside tea ceremonies and seasonal festivals that are still widely celebrated by both young and old.
[L] The key factor is not technology itself but whether communities and governments actively choose to protect their heritage and pass it on.

[Conc] In conclusion, while technology speeds up cultural change, it does not have to kill traditions. With conscious effort, public support, and creative use of digital tools, societies can welcome new technology while keeping their identity and passing it on to the next generation.

## 4.2 Problem / Solution

**Prompt**: International tourism damages local culture. What can be done to minimise the negative impact?

International tourism brings economic benefits, but it also raises concerns about cultural damage. This essay will discuss the main problems and suggest solutions.

[T] One major problem is the commercialisation of tradition.
[E] When sacred ceremonies and crafts are performed mainly for tourists, they often lose their real meaning and become shallow performances aimed at making money.
[Ex] In some destinations, religious dances that once held deep spiritual meaning are now shortened and repeated daily for visitors.
[L] As a result, the very traditions that attract tourists are slowly hollowed out.

[T] Another problem is the pressure of large visitor numbers on small communities.
[E] Crowds damage historic sites and push local people out of their neighbourhoods through rising rents and constant noise.
[Ex] Cities such as Barcelona and Kyoto have seen residents leave because tourism has changed the character of their streets.
[L] When local people are forced out, the living culture that visitors came to experience disappears with them.

[T] To deal with this, governments could set up cultural protection zones and fund local artists.
[E] Visitor numbers at sensitive sites should also be controlled through booking systems.
[Ex] A case in point is Machu Picchu and parts of Venice, where timed entry has eased pressure on fragile heritage.
[L] Such measures keep cultural sites alive for both residents and future visitors.

[T] Another useful approach is community-based tourism, where local people decide how their culture is shown.
[E] The money earned should fund cultural preservation rather than flow to foreign tour operators.
[Ex] Village-run homestay programmes in parts of Thailand and Peru, for instance, let residents share meals, crafts, and stories on their own terms.
[L] When communities control the experience, tourism becomes a reason to keep traditions alive rather than a force that erodes them.

[Conc] In summary, reducing tourism's cultural impact needs regulation, community control, and visitor education. Managed carefully, tourism can strengthen local traditions rather than damage them.

# 5. Government

## 5.1 Agree / Disagree

**Prompt**: Governments should spend money on public services rather than the arts. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

[Para+Pos] Some people argue that government money should be spent only on essential public services. While I understand this priority, I believe arts funding is also a necessary investment that should not be cut entirely.

[T] There is a strong case for putting public services first.
[E] In many countries, hospitals do not have enough money and schools lack basic resources such as books, equipment, and qualified teachers.
[Ex] When people struggle to access basic healthcare or send their children to a decent school, spending on art exhibitions and concerts can seem irresponsible.
[L] From this view, scarce public money must be directed first to what keeps people alive and educated.

[T] However, cutting arts funding completely would be short-sighted.
[E] The arts contribute to the economy through tourism, creative industries, and design, all of which create jobs and bring in foreign income.
[Ex] Vienna earns large amounts of money each year from visitors drawn by its musical heritage and historic theatres. Cities like London and Paris show similar patterns, with their museums and galleries attracting millions of tourists.
[E] The arts also play an important role in mental health and national identity, giving people meaning, enjoyment, and a shared sense of belonging.
[L] A balanced approach is to keep modest arts funding while making sure essential services are fully resourced first.

[Conc] In conclusion, while public services must come first, a society that ignores the arts risks losing both cultural richness and significant economic benefits. The two are not in real conflict — they are both part of what a healthy and balanced society needs.

## 5.2 Problem / Solution

**Prompt**: The gap between the rich and the poor is increasing. What problems does this cause? What solutions can governments implement?

The growing gap between rich and poor is one of the most serious challenges facing modern societies. This essay will look at the key problems and suggest solutions.

[T] The first problem is that inequality limits access to good education and healthcare.
[E] When poor families cannot afford private schools or medical care, their children start life with fewer chances.
[Ex] In many American cities, for instance, children from low-income areas attend underfunded schools and have far lower university entry rates than peers nearby.
[L] In this way, inequality is passed down through generations rather than fixed by individual effort.

[T] Inequality also weakens social cohesion.
[E] When a small group enjoys most of the wealth, resentment builds up and can lead to higher crime and political instability.
[Ex] Several Latin American countries have seen this pattern, with rising tensions between rich and poor neighbourhoods.
[L] As the gap widens, the sense of shared society that holds a country together begins to break down.

[T] To deal with this, governments should use progressive taxation to fund social programmes.
[E] Higher earners pay a larger share, and the money supports those who need help most through healthcare and housing.
[Ex] A case in point is the Nordic countries, where high top tax rates fund universal services and keep inequality among the lowest.
[L] Fair taxation turns private wealth into shared public benefit.

[T] In addition, investment in education and job training is crucial.
[E] Vocational courses help adults move into better-paid work, and minimum wage laws make sure growth reaches workers at the bottom.
[Ex] Germany's apprenticeship system, for instance, gives young people a clear route into skilled jobs.
[L] Combined with fair taxation, these policies give people real tools to climb the ladder.

[Conc] In summary, income inequality damages education, health, and social harmony. Through fair taxation, education investment, and worker protections, governments can build a fairer society.

# 6. Crime

## 6.1 Causes / Effects

**Prompt**: Youth crime rates are increasing. What are the causes? What effects does this have on society?

[Para] Youth crime rates have gone up in many countries in recent years, which worries policymakers, parents, and the wider public. This essay will examine the main causes of this trend and its effects on society.

[T] Several factors contribute to rising youth crime.
[E] Family breakdown is a significant cause: children from unstable homes, where parents are often absent or in conflict, are statistically more likely to get involved in criminal behaviour.
[Ex] A lack of parental supervision, combined with constant exposure to violent media, can easily lead young people in the wrong direction at an impressionable age.
[E] In addition, poverty plays a key role, as young people in deprived areas with few opportunities may turn to crime out of frustration or simply to get money.
[L] Together, broken families and limited opportunities form the soil in which youth crime grows.

[T] The effects on society are wide-ranging and often long-lasting.
[E] Youth crime threatens public safety, puts a heavy burden on the police and justice system, and keeps a cycle of harm going when young offenders are not given a real chance to reform.
[Ex] Communities with high youth crime rates also suffer from falling property values and less business investment, which makes the original problems even worse over time.
[E] Families of both victims and offenders often pay a long emotional and financial price, and trust between neighbours can break down over time.
[L] In this way, the harm caused by young offenders spreads far beyond the original crime and weakens society as a whole.

[Conc] In conclusion, youth crime is driven mainly by family instability and poverty. Its effects reach far beyond individual victims and damage entire communities, which is why prevention through better support for families, schools, and young people is essential.

## 6.2 Discuss Both Views

**Prompt**: Some think longer prison sentences reduce crime. Others believe there are better methods. Discuss both views.

[Para] The best way to reduce crime is an ongoing debate. Some people support longer prison sentences as a deterrent, while others argue that other strategies are more effective.
[Pos] This essay will look at both sides before giving my own view, which favours a mix of fair sentencing and prevention rather than longer sentences alone.

[T] Supporters of harsher sentencing argue that the threat of long prison terms discourages crime.
[E] When potential criminals know that they could spend many years behind bars, they are more likely to think twice before breaking the law.
[Ex] Keeping repeat offenders in prison for longer also directly protects the public by taking dangerous people off the streets.
[L] From this view, tougher sentences are a clear and simple way to reduce crime.

[T] However, research suggests that longer sentences on their own are not enough to reduce crime in the long run.
[E] Many prisoners reoffend soon after release because prison does not deal with the root causes of crime, such as addiction, mental illness, and lack of education or job skills.
[E] Long sentences can even backfire by exposing young offenders to hardened criminals, turning small offenders into more serious ones who are harder to reform later.
[Ex] In several countries, prisons are now described as "crime schools" for exactly this reason, and reoffending rates remain high despite tough sentencing.
[L] In other words, locking people away for longer does not automatically make society safer once they come out.

[Conc] In my view, a mix of fair sentencing and prevention offers the best solution. Investment in prison education, mental health support, and community rehabilitation deals with the underlying causes of crime rather than just punishing the symptoms after the damage has been done.

# 7. Animals

## 7.1 Discuss Both Views

**Prompt**: Some think animal experimentation is acceptable. Others believe it is wrong. Discuss both views.

[Para] The ethics of animal experimentation are widely debated. While some see it as a necessary step in medical progress, others view it as cruel and outdated. This essay will examine both sides before giving my own view.

[T] Those who support animal testing argue that it has been key to developing life-saving medicines and vaccines.
[E] Many treatments for cancer, heart disease, and infectious diseases were first tested on animals before being given to humans, and without this step medical progress would have been much slower.
[Ex] The development of vaccines, including those used in the recent pandemic, relied heavily on animal trials at early stages.
[L] From this view, animal testing has saved countless human lives and should not be abandoned lightly.

[T] On the other side, opponents argue that animals have a basic right to live free from suffering and should not be used as tools for human research.
[E] Biological differences mean that test results on animals are not always reliable for humans, so some experiments cause real harm to animals without producing genuinely useful data.
[Ex] With advances in technology, alternatives such as computer modelling, organ-on-a-chip systems, and cell culture testing are becoming more useful and accurate every year.
[L] These alternatives suggest that the old reasons for animal testing are slowly losing their strength.

[Conc] In my view, the scientific community has a responsibility to use alternatives wherever possible, and to reduce suffering where animal testing is still needed. The goal should be gradual reduction rather than an immediate ban, which would risk slowing down important medical research that benefits both humans and animals.

## 7.2 Problem / Solution

**Prompt**: Many species are becoming extinct. What are the causes? What can be done?

The loss of plant and animal species is one of the most worrying environmental trends today. This essay will look at the main causes and suggest solutions.

[T] The main cause of species loss is habitat destruction.
[E] Forests are cleared for farming and timber, wetlands drained for housing, and oceans polluted by plastic and chemicals.
[Ex] The Amazon, for example, loses large areas every year to cattle ranching.
[L] When wildlife is left with nowhere to live, extinction becomes a matter of time.

[T] Illegal poaching is another major driver.
[E] Animals such as elephants, tigers, and rhinos continue to be hunted to supply illegal markets for ivory, fur, and traditional medicine.
[Ex] A case in point is the African elephant, whose population has fallen sharply because of demand for ivory in parts of Asia.
[L] As long as profits from poaching remain high, even protected species will keep disappearing.

[T] To deal with this, governments must strengthen environmental laws and expand protected areas.
[E] International cooperation is essential for migratory species and shared oceans, since no single country can protect them alone.
[Ex] Agreements such as the international ban on the ivory trade have already helped slow the decline of elephants.
[L] Coordinated rules across borders give species a real chance of recovery.

[T] At the individual level, consumers can support sustainable products that do not damage natural habitats.
[E] Choosing certified timber, seafood, and palm oil signals that customers care about how products are made.
[Ex] Boycotts of products linked to deforestation have already pushed major brands to change supply chains.
[L] Together with stronger laws, these everyday choices add up to real pressure on the industries driving species loss.

[Conc] In summary, habitat destruction and poaching are driving species loss at an alarming rate. Through stronger laws, international cooperation, and individual choices, this trend can still be slowed.

# 8. Environment

## 8.1 Agree / Disagree

**Prompt**: Environmental problems are too big for individuals to solve. Only governments and large companies can make a difference. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

[Para+Pos] Some people argue that environmental problems are so large that only big institutions can deal with them. While I agree that government action is essential, I also believe individual contributions play a meaningful role and cannot be dismissed.

[T] Government action is clearly important for large-scale environmental issues.
[E] Only governments can pass emissions laws, regulate factories, or set up protected areas for wildlife.
[Ex] Moving to renewable energy, for example, needs infrastructure investment in power grids and storage systems that is far beyond what individuals can provide.
[L] In this sense, dismissing the role of governments would be unrealistic, since some problems can only be solved at the national or international level.

[T] However, dismissing individual action as unimportant is both inaccurate and harmful.
[E] Consumer choices shape markets when they add up across millions of people, and businesses pay attention to what their customers want.
[Ex] The growth of the organic food industry and the switch to electric vehicles were driven mainly by individual demand, not by government orders. Grassroots movements have also repeatedly pushed governments into greener policies that they would not have introduced on their own.
[L] When millions of people make small changes — using less plastic, eating less meat, or saving energy at home — the combined impact is significant and can shift entire industries.

[Conc] In conclusion, the most effective approach combines top-down regulation with bottom-up changes in behaviour, because neither one alone is enough to deal with problems on the scale of climate change and pollution. Both governments and citizens need to act together.

## 8.2 Causes / Solutions

**Prompt**: What are the causes of global warming? What solutions can be implemented?

Global warming is a serious threat to the planet, affecting weather, food, and human health. This essay will look at its main causes and suggest solutions.

[T] The biggest cause is burning fossil fuels for energy.
[E] Coal, oil, and gas power most of the world's electricity, transport, and heating, releasing large amounts of CO2.
[Ex] A case in point is China's heavy reliance on coal-fired power plants, which still produce a large share of its electricity.
[L] As long as the world's energy comes mainly from fossil fuels, emissions will keep pushing temperatures higher.

[T] Deforestation is the second major cause.
[E] When forests are cleared for farming or timber, the planet loses its main natural way of absorbing carbon.
[Ex] Large-scale agriculture, especially cattle and rice farming, also adds methane emissions to the problem.
[L] In this way, the same activities that feed a growing population are weakening the planet's defences.

[T] The most urgent step is moving from fossil fuels to renewable energy.
[E] Governments should set strict emission targets, use carbon pricing, and invest heavily in solar, wind, and hydroelectric power.
[Ex] Germany's Energiewende programme, for instance, has turned renewables into a major share of national electricity within one generation.
[L] Such a shift attacks the largest source of emissions directly and shows that a low-carbon economy is realistic.

[T] Planting trees and protecting existing forests are equally important.
[E] At the individual level, energy-saving habits, eating less meat, and using public transport all help when adopted widely.
[Ex] Costa Rica, for example, has nearly doubled its forest cover through long-term reforestation programmes.
[L] Together with the shift to renewables, these efforts can reduce new emissions and pull existing carbon out of the air.

[Conc] In summary, global warming is mainly driven by burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests. A mix of regulation, innovation, and individual responsibility offers the best way forward.

# 9. Equality

## 9.1 Discuss Both Views

**Prompt**: Women joining the workforce equally is positive. Some think it has negative effects on family life. Discuss both views.

[Para] The growing participation of women in the workforce is widely seen as an important social achievement. However, some argue that it has come at the cost of family stability. This essay will look at both views.

[T] Those who view women's workforce participation positively point to financial independence, career growth, and economic gains for society as a whole.
[E] Working women are no longer financially dependent on their husbands, which gives them more freedom and protection from abusive relationships.
[Ex] Families with two incomes also enjoy higher living standards and can offer their children more opportunities in education and travel. Children who see mothers building careers may develop more open and equal attitudes towards gender roles.
[L] From this view, women joining the workforce is a clear gain for individuals, families, and the wider economy.

[T] Critics, however, argue that when both parents work full time, childcare can suffer in important ways.
[E] Young children benefit from regular parental time during their early years, and outside childcare, however professional, cannot fully replace the daily bonding between parent and child.
[Ex] Some studies suggest that children whose parents have very limited time at home report feeling less emotionally supported, especially during stressful periods at school or in their teenage years.
[L] For these critics, the cost of dual-income family life falls most heavily on the children, which is why the concern cannot be dismissed.

[Conc] In conclusion, women's equal participation in the workforce is overall very positive, but societies must invest in support systems that allow families to thrive alongside professional life rather than asking women to choose between career and family.

## 9.2 Causes / Solutions

**Prompt**: The gender pay gap persists. Why does this happen? What can be done?

Despite decades of progress, a clear pay gap between men and women still exists in most countries. This essay will look at the main causes and suggest solutions.

[E] Women are over-represented in lower-paying sectors such as care work and teaching, while men dominate higher-paying industries such as finance and engineering.

[E] Women who take time off to look after children often miss promotions, while their male colleagues keep climbing the ladder.
[Ex] Many women report being passed over for projects after returning from maternity leave, even when their performance was strong before.

**Prompt**: Many people feel lonely and isolated in modern society. What are the causes? What measures can be taken?

[Para] Loneliness and social isolation have become more common in modern societies, affecting people of all ages. This essay will look at the main causes and suggest some measures.

[T] One main cause is the changing nature of work and living arrangements.
[E] Urbanisation has pulled people away from close-knit rural communities into cities where neighbours rarely interact.
[Ex] Long working hours and the rise of remote work further reduce real social contact, since many people now spend most of their day in front of a screen at home.
[L] As a result, modern lifestyles have quietly removed many of the daily encounters that used to keep people connected.

[T] Another important factor is demographic change.
[E] The growing number of single-person households, later marriage, smaller families, and ageing populations all add to the problem.
[Ex] Elderly people who have lost their spouses and live alone are especially at risk, particularly where adult children have moved away for work.
[L] These shifts in family structure mean that fewer people have a built-in network to fall back on.

[T] To deal with this, local governments could invest in community infrastructure that encourages people to meet.
[Ex] Parks, libraries, and neighbourhood centres give people natural reasons to leave home, and volunteer visiting programmes provide regular contact for the elderly.

[L] Small design choices, such as benches and small plazas, can have a real effect on how often people interact.

[Conc] In conclusion, modern loneliness comes from urbanisation, overwork, and digital disconnection. Tackling it needs deliberate investment in community life, thoughtful urban design, and targeted support for vulnerable groups like the elderly.
