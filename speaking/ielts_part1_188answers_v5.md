## 1. Food (Q1–Q4)

**Q1. What is your favourite food?**
I'd say my favourite would be home-cooked stir-fries — nothing fancy, just simple Sichuan-style dishes. Growing up in Yibin, I was surrounded by spicy food, so I've always had a high chilli tolerance. When I was younger I lived on instant noodles, but these days I appreciate a proper home-cooked meal. My wife's a pretty good cook, which helps.

**Q2. What kind of food did you like when you were young?**
That would be instant noodles and spicy snacks, like most kids in Sichuan. My grandfather used to joke that I'd eat chilli on everything, even fruit. I could have instant noodles every day without getting bored. Looking back, my diet was terrible, but at the time I thought it was the best thing in the world.

**Q3. Do you eat different foods at different times of the year?**
Yeah, definitely. In winter, hotpot is almost a weekly thing — Chengdu is famous for it, and it's perfect for cold days. In summer, we go for lighter stuff like cold noodles or fruit. It's typical for Chinese families to eat seasonally, and living in Sichuan makes it even more noticeable because the food culture here is so strong.

**Q4. Has your favourite food changed since you were a child?**
Oh, completely. I went from instant noodles to actually caring about nutrition. Since becoming a dad, I try to set a good example for my son — I can't tell him to eat vegetables if I'm eating junk food. My wife cooks most of our meals now, and I've come to appreciate that. Maybe it'll shift again as he gets older.

**Q75. Would you like to work as a teacher in a rule-free school?**

Chengdu has been one of China's fastest-developing cities, and I've had a front-row seat to that transformation.

**Q178. Big changes?**
Huge. The metro expanded from two or three lines to one of the most extensive networks in the country. New tech parks, cultural venues everywhere. The speed impresses visitors constantly. Sometimes I revisit areas I knew well and barely recognise them. Development here is relentless.

**Q179. Friendly to children and old people?**
Getting better. Since becoming a dad, I notice playgrounds, parks, family facilities more. Chengdu has invested heavily. For elderly people, the fast pace and digital-first nature can be challenging — my grandfather would struggle here. But public transport and healthcare are good. Progress is real.

**Q180. Would you recommend it?**
Yes, with an honest caveat. Great for tech careers, and the lifestyle is more relaxed than Beijing or Shanghai. The food alone is worth it — Sichuan cuisine is incredible. But costs have risen, competition is intense, and work-life balance takes effort. I'd tell ambitious young people: come prepared and enjoy the hotpot.

## 39. Day off (Q181–Q184)

**Q181. When was your last few days off?**
Spring Festival — we went to Yibin to visit my grandparents. My son explored the vegetable garden with my grandfather, fascinated by the wood-burning stove. He watched my grandmother cook, asked a hundred questions. Going back always feels like stepping into a slower, simpler world. Exactly what a holiday should be.

**Q182. What do you usually do on days off?**
Family activities — parks, the zoo, or wandering new parts of Chengdu with my son. Bad weather means a mall. Sometimes my wife and I take turns so the other gets solo hours. I might use that time for photography practice or reading. But the default is family-centred. Old friends sometimes join us if they're visiting Chengdu.

**Q183. Days off with parents or friends?**
Almost always with my wife and son. My grandparents are in Yibin — visiting requires a proper trip. We manage occasional group outings, but most days off are us three. When my grandfather visits Chengdu, those are special occasions — my son is always overjoyed.

**Q184. What would you do if you had a day off tomorrow?**
Sleep in first — no alarm, no kindergarten run. Then take my son to explore somewhere new in Chengdu — maybe a park we keep meaning to visit. Bring my camera and practise photography while he explores. A day with no plan, no rush. That's my ideal — and surprisingly rare in my current life.

## 40. Dreams (Q185–Q188)

**Q185. Can you remember your dreams?**
Hardly ever. Most fade within seconds — I might remember a feeling but the story vanishes. I think my brain is too occupied with work problems. My son, by contrast, remembers his in vivid detail every morning — dragons, flying LEGO castles, his teacher turning into a dinosaur. His imagination is something I genuinely envy.

**Q186. Do you share your dreams with others?**
Occasionally, if something funny sticks. I might mention it to my wife at breakfast. My son tells me about his dreams every morning in extraordinary detail — entire adventures that would make great sci-fi plots. He's shy about most things, but absolutely confident when narrating his dream stories. It's one of my favourite morning moments.

**Q187. Do you think dreams have special meanings?**
Mixed feelings. As someone who works with logic all day, I think dreams are just your brain processing information. But I understand why people find meaning in them — they feel intensely real. It's fascinating even from a scientific perspective. Inception, one of my favourite films, explored this beautifully — the idea that dreams can feel more real than reality.

**Q188. Do you want to make your dreams come true?**
Life dreams, absolutely. My biggest one is becoming an independent researcher — working deeply on problems I care about, with freedom and autonomy. A quieter life, like what my grandfather has in Yibin, but applied to intellectual work. It's not a grand public ambition, but it's deeply personal. And of course, being present for my son as he grows up — that's the most important dream of all.
