# 题1：擅长做计划的人 【人物A·张伟】

**P2**

I'd like to talk about my college friend Zhang Wei. We both studied computer science at university, and we've known each other for nearly twenty years. He's now a technical director at an AI startup.

He is by far the most organized person I know. Every January, he creates a comprehensive annual plan covering work objectives, learning goals, fitness targets, and family travel — all broken down into weekly tasks inside a tool called Notion. What really sets him apart is that he actually reviews his progress every Sunday evening and adjusts the plan if needed. Most people make New Year's resolutions and forget them by February, but Zhang Wei treats his plans like engineering projects.

The most impressive example was when he decided to switch jobs two years ago. He didn't just fire off applications. Instead, he mapped out a six-month roadmap: the first month was dedicated to reviewing core algorithms, the second to practising coding interviews, and the third to researching target companies and sending tailored applications. He even scheduled mock interviews with friends every weekend. By the end of the process, he had three competing offers and chose his ideal role at an AI company.

His approach has genuinely influenced me. I used to only plan within work projects — deadlines and milestones, that sort of thing. But after watching Zhang Wei, I started using a simple kanban board to organize my personal life too — weekend family activities, books to read, even household tasks. I'm still nowhere near as disciplined as he is, but the improvement has been noticeable.

I think what I admire most is not just the planning itself, but his consistency. Anyone can make a plan; very few people actually follow through.

**P3**

**Q: Do you think it's important to plan ahead?**
Definitely. In my experience working in the tech industry, projects without clear plans almost always run into problems — missed deadlines, duplicated effort, confused priorities. But it's not just about work. Even in daily life, a simple plan for the weekend makes everything smoother, especially when you have a young child. Without a plan, the whole day can slip away doing nothing meaningful.

**Q: What activities do we need to plan ahead?**
Anything with real consequences if it goes wrong — travel, career moves, financial decisions, major purchases. You wouldn't buy an apartment on impulse. But even smaller things like dinner parties or weekend trips benefit from some preparation. My friend Zhang Wei's career switch worked precisely because he planned every step rather than just hoping for the best.

**Q: Do you think children should plan their future careers?**
Not at a young age. My son is five — he says he wants to be a "dinosaur expert" one week and a "LEGO builder" the next. That kind of free exploration is healthy and should be encouraged. What children can learn, though, is basic time management — finishing one task before starting another, putting toys away before dinner. Those small habits build the foundation for planning later in life.

**Q: Should children ask their teachers or parents for advice when making plans?**
Both perspectives are valuable. Teachers understand a child's academic abilities and classroom behaviour, while parents know their personality, interests, and emotional needs. Ideally, a child gathers input from both and gradually learns to weigh different opinions. I think the goal should be teaching children how to make decisions, not making decisions for them.

**Q: Is making study plans popular among young people?**
It's growing, partly thanks to apps like Notion and the popular "study with me" videos on platforms like Bilibili. But there's often a gap between planning and execution. Many young people spend more time designing beautiful study schedules than actually studying. The tool isn't the problem — consistency and discipline are what matter most.

**Q: Do you think choosing a college major is closely related to a person's future career?**
There's a connection, but it's far from absolute. I studied computer science and ended up in the internet industry, so for me the link was direct. But I know people who studied literature and now work in tech marketing, or who studied engineering and switched to finance. What matters more than your major is your willingness to keep learning and adapt to new opportunities as they arise.

# 题2：想见的名人 【Linus Torvalds】

**P2**

The famous person I'd most like to meet is Linus Torvalds, the creator of the Linux operating system. My friend Zhang Wei and I sometimes discuss who has had the biggest influence on our careers, and for me the answer is always Linus.

I first learned about him during an operating systems class at university. Our professor explained that Torvalds had written the Linux kernel while still a college student in Finland — entirely on his own, as a personal project. As a computer science student myself at the time, I found that absolutely astonishing. The idea that a single individual could create something that would eventually power the majority of the world's servers, smartphones, and supercomputers seemed almost unreal.

Over the past fourteen years working in the internet industry, nearly every system I've touched has run on Linux. The servers, the deployment tools, the development environments — Linux is the invisible foundation beneath everything. So in a very real sense, my entire professional life has been built on his work.

If I could meet him, I'd want it to be in a casual setting — maybe a small tech conference or even a coffee shop. I'd ask him how he found the motivation to keep developing Linux as an open-source project when there was no financial incentive in the early years. I'd also love to hear his thoughts on how operating systems might evolve in the age of artificial intelligence.

What I admire most about Torvalds isn't just his technical brilliance — it's his philosophy. By making Linux open-source, he created a model where millions of developers worldwide could use, improve, and share code freely. That spirit of open collaboration has fundamentally shaped the internet and the entire technology industry. It's a reminder that the most impactful contributions aren't always the ones you get paid for.

**P3**

**Q: What are the advantages and disadvantages of being a famous child?**
On the positive side, famous children often gain access to exceptional resources — the best schools, mentors, and financial support. However, the downsides can be severe. They lose privacy at an age when they should be free to make mistakes without public scrutiny. The pressure to maintain their image can cause serious anxiety and loneliness. I wouldn't want that for my own son — I'd rather he has a normal, happy childhood.

**Q: What can today's children do to become famous?**
Social media has dramatically lowered the barrier. Children can post singing, dancing, or creative videos online and potentially reach millions overnight. Talent shows and reality TV are other paths. However, I personally think fame should never be a goal for children. Their focus should be on learning, playing, and growing. Fame pursued too early often comes at the cost of a normal development.

**Q: What can children do with their fame?**
Some use it positively — raising awareness for causes, inspiring other children, or funding their education. But managing fame requires maturity that most children simply don't have. Without careful guidance from parents, fame can lead to exploitation, unrealistic expectations, and emotional burnout. The responsibility falls heavily on the adults around them.

**Q: Do people become famous because of their talent?**
In fields like science and technology, talent combined with hard work is usually the primary driver. Linus Torvalds became influential purely through his technical genius. But in entertainment, factors like appearance, marketing, social connections, and sheer luck often matter as much as — or more than — talent. The path to fame varies enormously depending on the field.

**Q: Is it easy to become famous in your country?**
Getting noticed has become easier with platforms like Douyin, where a single video can go viral overnight. But genuine, lasting fame — built on real achievement and sustained contribution — remains extremely rare. Most viral sensations fade within weeks. There's a big difference between being temporarily trending and being truly respected.

**Q: Do you want to be a famous person?**
Honestly, not at all. I value my quiet life — reading a sci-fi novel after my son goes to bed, cycling through a Kyoto backstreet, thinking deeply about a technical problem. Fame would disrupt all of that. I believe the richest life is one lived with depth and purpose, not one lived in the spotlight. My ambition is to become a thoughtful researcher, not a public figure.

# 题3：机智解决问题的人 【人物A·张伟】

**P2**

I want to talk about Zhang Wei again — specifically about his remarkable ability to solve problems by thinking differently from everyone else.

The best example happened at his company right before a major online sales event, similar to Singles' Day. Their e-commerce platform suddenly became extremely slow — pages were taking ten or fifteen seconds to load, and the event was starting in just a few hours. The entire engineering team was in crisis mode.

Most of the engineers immediately dove into the codebase, searching for bugs or performance bottlenecks. They spent several hours combing through thousands of lines of code, running diagnostics, and checking database queries. Nothing turned up. The pressure was mounting — if the website wasn't fixed before the sale began, the company would lose millions in revenue.

Zhang Wei took a completely different approach. Instead of looking at the code, he started analysing the network traffic logs — something nobody else had thought to check. Within thirty minutes, he discovered that a third-party CDN node had malfunctioned. This meant that a huge portion of user requests were being routed to the most geographically distant server, creating massive latency. The code was perfectly fine — the problem was in the infrastructure layer.

He immediately wrote a script to redirect all traffic to a backup CDN node. Within ten minutes, the website was running smoothly again. Crisis averted. After the event, he went further and designed an automated monitoring system that would detect similar CDN failures in the future and switch over automatically, without any human intervention.

What makes Zhang Wei truly smart isn't raw intelligence — it's his ability to step back when everyone else is tunnelling in one direction and ask, "What if the problem isn't where we're all looking?"

**P3**

**Q: Do you think children are born smart or they learn to become smart?**
I believe intelligence is primarily developed through experience and education, though some children do show natural curiosity earlier than others. Critical thinking, problem-solving, and creativity are skills that grow with practice — they aren't fixed at birth. Zhang Wei wasn't born knowing how to debug servers. He built that ability through years of deliberate learning and real-world challenges.

**Q: How do children become smart at school?**
Through hands-on projects and genuine problem-solving, not just memorising textbooks and passing exams. When children are encouraged to ask questions, experiment, and figure things out on their own — even if they fail initially — that's when real intellectual growth happens. The best teachers create environments where curiosity is rewarded, not punished.

**Q: Why are some people well-rounded and others only good at one thing?**
It often depends on exposure and personal inclination. Some people are naturally curious about many fields and enjoy variety. Others find one area fascinating and pour all their energy into it. In the tech industry, we value what's called a "T-shaped" profile — deep expertise in one specific area combined with broad understanding across related fields. Both types of people contribute differently to a team.

**Q: Why does modern society need talents of all kinds?**
Because the problems we face today are complex and interconnected. Building a successful app, for example, requires programmers, designers, psychologists, marketers, and business strategists working together. No single type of talent is sufficient. Diversity of skills and perspectives is what drives innovation and helps solve problems that no individual could tackle alone.

**Q: Do you think smart children are happier than other children?**
Not necessarily — in fact, sometimes the opposite. Academically gifted children often face higher expectations from parents and teachers, which creates pressure and anxiety. Some feel isolated because their interests differ from their peers. I think happiness comes more from emotional support, freedom to explore, and strong relationships than from raw intelligence. That's why my only expectation for my son is to be healthy, happy, and safe.

**Q: Is it important for schools to identify and develop each student's talents?**
Very much so. Every child has different strengths — some are analytical, some are creative, some are athletic, some are empathetic. A good education system recognises this diversity and provides multiple pathways rather than forcing everyone through identical curricula. One-size-fits-all education wastes enormous potential.

# 题4：朋友自学 【人物A·张伟自学AI】

**P2**

I'd like to describe Zhang Wei's experience teaching himself machine learning — completely without a formal teacher or training programme.

About two years ago, Zhang Wei's company began shifting its strategy toward artificial intelligence. He was a strong traditional software developer, but he had no background in machine learning or data science. Rather than waiting for the company to arrange training, he decided to take matters into his own hands.

His approach was impressively systematic. He started with Andrew Ng's well-known machine learning course on Coursera, working through every lecture, quiz, and programming assignment over about two months. Then he moved on to practical application, finding open-source machine learning projects on GitHub and studying their code, modifying it, and building small projects of his own.

Every evening after work, he dedicated two to three hours to studying. When he encountered concepts he didn't understand — and there were many, especially around the mathematics of neural networks — he'd search Stack Overflow, read research papers, or watch explanation videos on Bilibili. Without a teacher to ask, he developed an extraordinary ability to find answers independently.

The whole process took about a year. By the end of it, he had built enough knowledge and a strong enough portfolio of personal projects that he successfully transferred to his company's AI team. He now works on natural language processing, which ironically is also the field I'm most interested in.

I think learning without a teacher is harder in some ways — you lose the immediate feedback and structured curriculum. But it's also deeper, because you have to truly understand every concept rather than just following instructions. In our industry, where technology changes faster than any classroom can keep up with, this kind of self-directed learning ability is arguably the most valuable skill anyone can have.

**P3**

**Q: Is it necessary to keep learning after graduating from school?**
In my field, it's not optional — it's survival. The programming languages and frameworks I used ten years ago are already outdated. But even beyond tech, the world is changing so rapidly that continuous learning is essential in almost every profession. The degree you earn at twenty-two is just a starting point, not a destination.

**Q: Should teachers make learning in their classes fun?**
Yes, but "fun" should mean engaging and relevant, not necessarily entertaining. The best teachers I've had used real-world projects and relatable examples to bring abstract concepts to life. A boring lecture on database theory versus actually building a small database system — the latter teaches you ten times more and you actually enjoy it.

**Q: Do you think there are too many subjects for students to learn?**
In China, I think students — especially in middle and high school — are overloaded. They study many subjects simultaneously at a surface level rather than exploring fewer subjects deeply. I'd prefer a system that allows older students to focus on areas that genuinely interest them, while maintaining a broad foundation in earlier years.

**Q: Is it better to focus on a few subjects or to learn many subjects?**
For younger children, breadth is important because you don't know what will spark your passion until you try it. But from high school onwards, I think focus becomes more valuable. Deep expertise in one or two areas, combined with general literacy across others, tends to produce the most capable and fulfilled people. That's the model I've seen work best in the tech industry.

**Q: Do you think enterprises should provide training for their employees?**
Absolutely. My company has a technology-sharing programme and provides a learning budget for each employee. This benefits both sides — employees develop new skills and feel valued, while the company gets a more capable workforce. Companies that don't invest in training often lose their best people to competitors who do.

**Q: Do you think it is good for older adults to continue learning?**
Very much so. Learning keeps the mind sharp and provides a sense of purpose. My grandfather learned to use a smartphone in his seventies — it wasn't easy for him, but it connected him to our family in ways that wouldn't have been possible otherwise. He can now video-call us, watch my son's drawing videos, and even check the weather forecast. It genuinely improved his quality of life.

# 题5：喜欢画画的孩子 【人物B·儿子】

**P2**

The child I want to describe is my own son. He's five years old, and he's been passionate about drawing since he was about three. He's actually a fairly shy boy — he's quiet around strangers and takes time to warm up in new environments — but when he picks up a crayon, he transforms completely. Drawing seems to unlock a confidence in him that doesn't always show in other situations.

I first noticed his interest when his kindergarten teacher mentioned that during free playtime, while other children were running around or playing with blocks, he would quietly go to the drawing corner and sit there absorbed in his work. He draws almost every day — sometimes three or four pictures before bedtime.

His favourite subjects are dinosaurs and LEGO creations. He often combines them in wonderfully imaginative ways — a T-Rex piloting a spaceship, or a LEGO castle guarded by velociraptors. Our fridge at home is completely covered with his artwork, and we've framed a few of our favourites for the living room wall.

I think drawing gives him a way to express ideas he can't quite put into words — which is especially important for a shy child. Sometimes when he struggles to describe something, he'll say, "Wait, let me draw it for you," and suddenly his whole inner world becomes visible on paper.

My wife and I are very supportive, but we're careful not to push him. We don't want drawing to become a chore or a source of pressure. We buy him supplies, take him to occasional art workshops, and most importantly, we praise his effort and imagination rather than technical skill. Our only expectation for him is to be healthy, happy, and safe. If drawing brings him joy, that's more than enough.

**P3**

**Q: What is the right age for a child to learn drawing?**
Three or four is a lovely time to start, but the emphasis should be entirely on exploration and enjoyment, not on producing technically correct pictures. Young children should be free to scribble, experiment with colours, and draw whatever their imagination suggests. Pressuring them into formal instruction too early risks turning a natural joy into a stressful obligation.

**Q: Why do most children draw more often than adults do?**
Children haven't developed the self-critical voice that holds adults back. They don't worry about whether their drawing looks "real" or "good enough" — they simply enjoy the process. Adults often stop drawing because they compare their work to professional artists and feel inadequate. It's a shame, because the creative benefits of drawing don't depend on skill level at all.

**Q: Why do some people visit galleries or museums instead of viewing artworks online?**
Standing in front of a real painting is a fundamentally different experience. You can see the texture of the brushstrokes, appreciate the actual scale, and sense the artist's physical presence in the work. A screen flattens all of that. When I take my son to exhibitions, he's always more engaged than when looking at the same images on a tablet — the physical environment adds something that technology can't replicate.

**Q: Do you think galleries and museums should be free of charge?**
For children, absolutely — early exposure to art helps shape their aesthetic sense and creative thinking. For adults, a reasonable fee is acceptable because it funds maintenance and new exhibitions. Some museums offer free days once a month, which is a good compromise that makes art accessible while maintaining financial sustainability.

**Q: How do artworks inspire people?**
Art shifts your perspective. After visiting an exhibition, I often notice colours, patterns, and details in everyday life that I would have overlooked before. For my son, seeing other children's artwork at school inspires him to try new subjects and techniques in his own drawings. Creativity feeds on exposure to other people's creativity.

**Q: What are the differences between reading a book and visiting a museum?**
Books engage your imagination through words — you build mental images and develop deep understanding over time. Museums engage your senses directly — you see, hear, and sometimes touch. They serve different but complementary purposes. I'd say books provide depth of understanding while museums provide breadth of sensory experience. Ideally, a well-rounded education includes both.

# 题6：乐于助人的人 【人物A·张伟助人】

**P2**

The person I want to describe is my friend Zhang Wei — this time focusing on his incredibly generous nature when it comes to helping other people.

About three years ago, Zhang Wei noticed that many fresh computer science graduates were struggling to find their first jobs. They had technical knowledge from university but no idea how to write a compelling résumé, handle a technical interview, or navigate the job market. So he created a WeChat group specifically for this purpose. He reviews their résumés, gives detailed feedback, and runs mock interview sessions on weekends — all completely free of charge. To date, he's helped well over a hundred young people.

But his generosity extends beyond the tech community. He also volunteers regularly at a community centre near his apartment, teaching elderly residents how to use smartphones. He shows them how to send WeChat messages, make video calls to their children and grandchildren, use mobile payment at shops, and even order food delivery. He's incredibly patient with them — even when someone asks the same question for the fifth time, he explains it again with a smile.

I once asked him why he invests so much personal time in helping others when his own work schedule is already demanding. He told me that when he was a fresh graduate himself, several senior colleagues went out of their way to guide and mentor him during a very difficult period. He feels a deep sense of obligation to pay that forward. He genuinely believes that small acts of help can change someone's entire trajectory.

I really admire this about him. In today's fast-paced world, most people — myself included — often feel too busy to help others beyond our immediate circle. Zhang Wei is a constant reminder that generosity doesn't require grand gestures or a lot of money. It just requires a willingness to share your time and knowledge with people who need it.

**P3**

**Q: What can children do to help their parents?**
Even young children can contribute in small but meaningful ways. My five-year-old helps tidy up his toys after playing, puts his plate in the sink after meals, and sometimes helps me carry light grocery bags. These tasks aren't about the practical help — they're about building a sense of responsibility and participation in family life. He feels proud when we thank him for helping.

**Q: Should children help their parents with household chores?**
Definitely. Age-appropriate chores teach responsibility, independence, and the understanding that a household runs on everyone's contribution. Children who never do chores may struggle with basic life skills when they eventually live on their own. It's not about burdening them — it's about preparing them for real life in a gentle, gradual way.

**Q: What kind of help do people need when looking for a new job?**
Practical help like résumé writing, interview preparation, and industry insights. Many graduates have solid technical skills but don't know how to present themselves effectively. Beyond that, emotional support is important too — job hunting can be lonely and demoralising, so having someone who believes in you and offers honest feedback makes a huge difference. That's exactly what Zhang Wei provides.

**Q: Who should people ask for help, colleagues or family members?**
It depends on the situation. For work-related problems, colleagues understand the professional context better and can offer more relevant advice. For life decisions — whether to relocate, change careers, or handle personal challenges — family members who know you deeply can provide emotional support and perspective that colleagues usually can't. The wisest approach is to consult both and weigh their input carefully.

**Q: Do you think schools should teach children to do household chores?**
Yes, I think it's valuable. Some schools in China have started including practical life skills in their curricula, and I support that. Not every child learns these skills at home — families have different situations and expectations. School-based life skills education ensures that all children develop a baseline of practical independence, regardless of their home environment.

**Q: Why are employees reluctant to ask their managers for help?**
Mostly fear of appearing incompetent or unable to handle their responsibilities. In many workplace cultures, there's an unspoken expectation that you should have everything figured out. Asking for help can feel like admitting failure. A good manager should actively create an environment where seeking guidance is seen as a sign of strength and self-awareness, not weakness.

Catchy pop songs from cartoons and children's shows are the most popular. My son constantly hums theme songs from his favourite dinosaur cartoon — simple, repetitive melodies that stick in your head. Music has the power to bring communities together, and that's worth investing in.

# 题8：重要的好朋友 【人物A·张伟友谊】

**P2**

The most important friend in my life is undoubtedly Zhang Wei. We met during our first year studying computer science at university — assigned to the same dormitory room by pure luck — and we've been close ever since. That's nearly twenty years of friendship.

What makes our friendship special isn't constant contact. We live in different parts of the country now, and we're both consumed by work and family responsibilities. But we make a point of having dinner together once a month — sometimes in person when schedules align, sometimes over a video call. And whenever something significant happens in either of our lives, the other person is always the first to know.

The moment that truly cemented Zhang Wei's importance was about five years ago, when I was agonising over whether to leave a safe, well-paying position at a large company for a smaller but fast-growing startup. My son had just been born, which made financial security feel more critical than ever. I was paralysed with indecision.

My wife was supportive but understandably cautious about our finances. My father's advice was straightforward — don't take the risk. But Zhang Wei approached it differently. He didn't just share his gut feeling. He actually researched the startup — its business model, its funding rounds, its leadership team — and gave me an honest, detailed assessment. He concluded that the company had genuine potential, but suggested I negotiate a strong compensation package to reduce downside risk.

Then he said something that changed everything: "You're in your thirties. If you don't take this chance now, you'll spend your forties wondering what if." That sentence broke through my paralysis. I made the switch, and it turned out to be one of the best decisions of my life.

A true friend isn't someone who tells you what you want to hear. It's someone who tells you what you need to hear and stands beside you regardless of the outcome.

**P3**

**Q: How do children make friends at school?**
Mostly through shared activities — sitting together in class, playing during breaks, collaborating on group projects. My son's closest friend at kindergarten is a boy who also loves dinosaurs. They bonded instantly over that shared interest. At that age, friendships form naturally around common enthusiasms rather than deliberate social effort.

**Q: How do children make friends when they are not at school?**
Through neighbourhood play, extracurricular classes, and family social events. My son met several friends at his art workshop — they draw together and compare their dinosaur creations. Parks and playgrounds are also natural meeting points. For shy children like my son, structured activities are often easier than completely unstructured play for making new connections.

**Q: Do you think it is better for children to have a few close friends or many casual friends?**
I strongly believe in a few close friends. Deep friendships teach children trust, loyalty, empathy, and conflict resolution — skills that superficial acquaintanceships simply can't develop. My own experience confirms this: I have many acquaintances but only two or three people I'd truly rely on in a crisis, and those relationships are infinitely more valuable.

**Q: Do you think a child's relationship with friends can be replaced by family?**
Not entirely. Family provides unconditional love and foundational security, but friends offer something fundamentally different — a relationship between equals, chosen freely. Friends help children develop independence, learn to navigate social dynamics, and discover who they are outside the family unit. Both types of relationship are essential for healthy development.

**Q: What are the differences between friends made inside and outside the workplace?**
Workplace friendships tend to revolve around shared tasks and professional context. They can be genuine, but they're often tested when one person leaves the company or when there's competition for a promotion. Friendships made outside work — through shared hobbies, university, or childhood — tend to be built on deeper personal connection and survive changes in circumstance more easily. Zhang Wei and I have stayed close through multiple job changes over fourteen years.

**Q: Do you think it's possible for bosses and employees to become friends?**
It's possible but requires careful navigation. The inherent power dynamic can create awkwardness — especially around sensitive topics like salary, performance evaluation, and promotion. # 题41：有用的书 【CSAPP】

**P2**

The book that has had the most profound and lasting impact on my professional life is "Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective" — commonly known as CSAPP. I read it in my second year of working as a programmer, and it fundamentally transformed how I understand and interact with computers.

I knew how to use the machine, but I didn't understand the machine.

CSAPP changed that completely. It explains — with remarkable clarity — how programs are compiled from source code into machine instructions, how the CPU actually executes those instructions, how memory is organized and managed, how data moves between cache, RAM, and disk, and why certain programming patterns are fast while others are slow. It connects the abstract world of software with the physical reality of hardware in a way that few other books achieve.

Reading it was like learning to drive versus understanding how an engine works. After CSAPP, I didn't just know that my code was slow — I understood why at a fundamental level and could fix it accordingly. I stopped writing code that merely worked and started writing code that worked efficiently, because I could now reason about what the machine was actually doing with my instructions.

More than a decade later, the principles I learned from CSAPP remain central to my daily work. When I encounter a performance issue, my debugging approach is still shaped by the mental models this book built. If I could recommend only one book to anyone studying or working in computer science, without hesitation it would be this one.

Transform them into essential community spaces that serve multiple functions — not just books, but co-working areas, children's programmes, digital literacy training for elderly residents, community meetings, and cultural events. **Q: Why do some children not like learning science at school?**

**Q: Should scientists explain the research process to the public?**
Absolutely. Public understanding of science builds trust, supports funding, and creates informed citizens who can distinguish genuine research from pseudoscience. When scientists communicate openly about their methods, uncertainties, and findings, it demystifies the research process and helps people understand that science is a self-correcting process, not a collection of final truths.

## 题46：花费超过预期 【椅子】

**P2**

I agonised for about fifteen minutes, mentally calculating the cost per hour of use. Ten hours a day, three hundred working days a year — the chair would cost about one RMB per hour of use over three years. When I framed it that way, spending more actually seemed rational rather than extravagant.

One year later, my back pain has largely resolved. I sit longer without discomfort, my posture has improved, and I'm more productive because I'm not constantly shifting to relieve pain. Zhang Wei was right: things you use for ten hours daily deserve serious investment.
