# 题1：完美工作 【独立研究者】

> **Describe a perfect job you would like to have in the future.**
> You should say:
> - What it is
> - How you knew about it
> - What it would be like
> - And explain why you think it would be perfect

**P2**

So, the perfect job for me would be an independent researcher in computer science—maybe systems design or distributed computing. I learned about this through reading "Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective," which showed me how elegant architecture can solve real problems. The work just really appeals to me. The main appeal is having complete autonomy over my research direction.

I imagine a quiet workspace, powerful computers, time to read academic papers, and the freedom to pursue questions just because they're intellectually interesting. Maybe collaborate occasionally with other researchers, but without corporate meetings or the constant pressure to ship products.

What makes it perfect is that it aligns with how I think. I've spent fourteen years in the internet industry—building systems, solving problems on tight deadlines—and while it's been valuable, I've craved deeper investigation. As a father and someone who values stability, I realize this dream might need to be partial: maybe a university position with research time.

Ultimately, the appeal is simple: asking important questions, having time to answer them properly, and contributing something that lasts beyond a quarterly release cycle. That kind of meaningful, autonomous work would feel genuinely perfect.

**P3**

**Q: What kinds of jobs do young people prefer?**

Well, young people today prefer creative and flexible roles—freelancing, startups, that kind of thing. They care more about meaning and autonomy than just salary. I'd say this generation saw their parents work endless hours without feeling fulfilled, so they're chasing passion projects. Technology made career paths possible that didn't even exist before.

**Q: What are the differences between the jobs that young people prefer and the jobs that older people prefer?**

Older people want stability—pension, health insurance, career ladder. Young people care about learning opportunities and work-life balance. Older workers stay at one company forever, whereas younger workers switch industries easily. Different economic realities, you know? Older people grew up in stable job markets, while young people adapted to volatility.

**Q: What factors should young people consider when choosing a job?**

I'd say personal values matter most, not just salary. Think carefully about learning opportunities since technology changes fast. Consider people and culture—you spend lots of time at work, toxic environments drain you badly. Financial stability matters too, don't ignore that. Think about whether you'll actually grow in the role.

**Q: What are the challenges faced by young people in finding a job?**

There's a real gap between education and job requirements. Universities teach theory, companies want practical experience and skills. Young people compete with experienced candidates and face discrimination. Without prestigious university connections or big city networks, the disadvantage is very significant and really real. Good mentors really help navigate this challenge.

# 题2：想见的名人 【Linus Torvalds】

> **Describe a famous person that you are interested in.**
> You should say:
> - Who this person is
> - How you knew about this person
> - What this person is famous for
> - And explain why you want to meet this person

**P2**

Linus Torvalds—he's the person I'd most like to meet. He's the Finnish software engineer who created Linux, the operating system that powers everything from Android phones to most servers worldwide. I first learned about him in university during my computer science degree, when we studied operating system design. Reading about how one person started Linux in 1991 as a hobby project—it completely changed how I thought about impact and scale.

What fascinates me is his relentless focus on technical excellence and his willingness to be bluntly honest about bad code, even when unpopular. He doesn't care about marketing—just quality work. For someone like me who values clear thinking and honest feedback, this is incredibly inspiring. Linux has influenced everything I've done in my fourteen-year tech career.

I'd want to meet him to understand how he maintains focus and judgment over decades, ignoring hype and staying true to engineering principles. He's remarkably humble despite his influence. I'm curious about whether he ever imagined Linux would become so central to global infrastructure. And honestly, I'd just want to hear his perspective on what makes good software architecture and good decision-making.

Meeting him would feel like learning from a master craftsman, not just a celebrity.

**P3**

**Q: What are the advantages and disadvantages of being a famous child?**

Being famous as a child has advantages—money, opportunities, great education. But honestly, the disadvantages are severe. A child didn't choose this, and suddenly everyone's judging their life. They lose privacy, they're defined by their parent's name, not their own choices. Peers get jealous, critics are harsh. I think the disadvantages outweigh benefits unless parents protect them really well.

**Q: What can today's children do to become famous?**

I'd say it's easier than ever now with social media. Kids can become famous through sports, music, streaming, TikTok trends. But here's the thing—social media fame is often hollow and comes with anxiety and addiction. Real, meaningful fame comes from genuine talent developed over years. It's harder but more sustainable than viral moments.

**Q: What can children do with their fame?**

Well, if a child's built genuine fame through talent, they can leverage it for good. Use their platform to support causes, help younger aspiring artists, mentor others. Some famous athletes become coaches. Musicians support arts education. The best outcome is when fame becomes a tool for contribution, not just consumption.

**Q: Do people become famous because of their talent?**

Not always, no. Luck and timing matter too, honestly. But sustainable fame—lasting for years—requires real talent. Linus is famous because he created something truly revolutionary and important. You can be briefly famous without talent, but staying relevant and respected absolutely requires genuine talent. It's essential for lasting influence.

**Q: Is it easy to become famous in your country?**

In China, it's easier than ever with Douyin and livestreaming. You can go viral overnight. But easy fame is often empty—millions of followers but no substance. Building real influence in tech, academia, business still takes years and genuine achievement. So it depends what kind of fame you mean, right?

**Q: Do you want to be a famous person?**

No, I don't, honestly. I value autonomy and privacy way too much. I'd rather do meaningful work that thoughtful people truly respect than be constantly interrupted. Fame distorts judgment—people worry about their image instead of truth. Excellent work and deep thinking matter much more than public attention and recognition.

# 题3：禁用手机的场合 【幼儿园家长会】

> **Describe an occasion when you were not allowed to use your mobile phone.**
> You should say:
> - When and where it was
> - Why you were not allowed to use your phone
> - What you wanted to do with your phone
> - And explain how you felt about it

**P2**

So my son's kindergarten held a parent-teacher conference about six months ago. The teachers had a simple rule: all parents must put their phones away. I felt a slight resistance at first—I had work messages, and I checked my phone constantly.

But once the phones went away, something shifted. The teachers discussed my son's development, his social interactions, his progress with numbers and letters. Without the phone as an escape, I actually listened. They mentioned he's quite shy and doesn't always participate in group activities, but when he focuses, his concentration is remarkable. He loves drawing, painting, building with blocks. He often works alone, which is normal at his age.

I found myself asking real questions, leaning forward, genuinely curious. My wife did the same. We learned details we'd have missed if distracted. The teachers noticed our engagement and became more open.

Afterwards, I felt relief. Not having the phone for ninety minutes felt lighter, not restrictive. I realized I'd been checking work messages out of habit, not necessity. The whole experience made me question how much time I was actually present with my family at home. That small rule—no phones—made the meeting feel real in a way that would have been impossible otherwise.

**P3**

**Q: Why do some people think it is necessary to ban the use of mobile phones in certain places?**

Phones are incredibly distracting, right? They fragment attention in classrooms, hospitals, everywhere. But beyond noise, there's something deeper—when people use phones around you, it signals that the screen matters more than you do. I think bans exist because distraction spreads. If others are distracted, it makes focus harder for everyone. In places where presence matters, phones genuinely interfere.

**Q: What are the disadvantages of using mobile phones in public places?**

Obviously there's noise and disruption. But here's the subtle part—it affects social bonds. Parents on phones in parks mean children lose attention. People on phones in restaurants have shallower conversations. There's safety issues too—people walk into traffic distracted. Most importantly, it's created a culture where ignoring people around you is acceptable. This erodes how we share spaces.

**Q: Do you think schools should ban the use of mobile phones?**

Absolutely, at least during classes and study time. School is for learning and building social skills. When students have phones, they're not present neurologically. Phones also create equal conditions—wealthy kids won't have advantages based on technology. Teenagers struggle with impulse control, so removing temptation is kind. Allow them in hallways maybe, but not classrooms.

**Q: How has mobile phone use changed people's lives?**

Phones gave us freedom and connection—work anywhere, reach anyone, access information instantly. This is genuinely revolutionary. But we've become constantly "on," always reachable. Work and rest blur together. People forgot how to be bored, and boredom is where creativity happens. Phones themselves are neutral—it's our relationship to them that changed.

# 题4：给别人建议 【给前同学职业建议】

> **Describe a time when you gave advice to someone.**
> You should say:
> - Who you gave the advice to
> - What the situation was
> - What advice you gave
> - And explain how the person responded

**P2**

About five years ago, a college friend reached out asking about his career. He'd been in IT at a bank for eight years, doing stable technical work. He was frustrated—growth had plateaued, the work felt repetitive, and he was earning less than he could elsewhere. His instinct was to quit immediately.

I'd seen people burn bridges that way, only to struggle in interviews because they'd been out of the job market for years. I suggested a different approach: "Use the next six months strategically. Pick a technology area—data engineering, cloud architecture, AI—and learn it deeply while still employed. Build a portfolio project. Get good at interviews. Then apply with confidence."

He took it seriously. He chose cloud technologies, did an online course, and built a real system on AWS. He practiced coding interviews consistently. After six months, he applied and got strong offers from top tech companies at significantly higher salaries.

What impressed me was how the systematic approach changed his thinking about career decisions. He realized planning before moving was less risky and more powerful than reactive job-hopping. I was proud my advice helped, and it reinforced my belief that thoughtful preparation beats desperate action.

**P3**

**Q: Should people prepare before giving advice?**

Absolutely. Unprepared advice can do real harm. The person asking is vulnerable, confused, looking for guidance. If you give quick opinions without thinking, you might send them wrong. Preparation means listening carefully to understand the real problem, not just the surface. You need to think through multiple perspectives, not just your own experience.

**Q: Is it good to ask advice from strangers online?**

It's dangerous, honestly. Online strangers don't know your full context, your values, or constraints. They give advice based on their own biases. You might get contradictory opinions and feel more confused. But it can be one input among many. Strangers offer perspectives you'd never hear locally. The problem is treating online advice as gospel. I'd say ask strangers but verify with people who actually know you.

**Q: What are the personalities of people whose job is to give advice?**

Professional advisors tend to be deeply patient listeners. They ask clarifying questions rather than assuming. Good advisors have humility—they recognize they can't know all answers, so they help people discover answers themselves. They're non-judgmental. They also have strong integrity because people only trust advice from honest people. They're curious about people rather than eager to push their own solutions.

**Q: What are the problems if you ask too many people for advice?**

You become paralyzed. I've seen people ask everyone they know before deciding, ending up confused with ten different opinions. Too much advice creates noise, not clarity. You get influenced by whoever speaks most convincingly, not whoever has best judgment. If you ask someone for advice then ignore it, they feel hurt. Also, constantly seeking advice is often just avoiding responsibility.

**Q: Why do some people think it's better to ask friends than parents?**

Friends understand your generation and current circumstances better. Parents sometimes give advice based on outdated assumptions. Friends feel less judgmental—parents worry about your choices or push their values. There's a power dynamic too—easier to tell a friend "I disagree" than tell your parent that. But honestly, the best approach is asking both. Parents have life experience and genuine concern, friends understand your actual situation.

**Q: When would old people ask young people for advice?**

About technology, definitely. Young people understand new tools intuitively in ways older people don't. Older people also ask about social trends and what's popular. Sometimes they ask about relationships or parenting because younger people have more progressive perspectives. Older people increasingly ask young people about tech careers because their own experience might be obsolete now.

# 题6：擅长做计划的人 【张伟】

> **Describe a person who is good at making plans.**
> You should say:
> - Who this person is
> - How you knew this person
> - What plans this person makes
> - And explain why you think he/she is good at planning

**P2**

Zhang Wei is my closest friend from university—we've known each other for nearly twenty years. He's someone I genuinely admire because of his remarkable ability to plan and execute complex goals. His planning discipline has shaped his life in impressive ways.

Zhang Wei is obsessed with planning. He uses Notion to maintain annual, quarterly, and monthly roadmaps and does weekly reviews every Sunday evening. What strikes me is that he treats plans as working documents that guide every decision, not just motivational exercises.

The best example is his career transition into artificial intelligence. About three years ago, his company shifted toward AI, and suddenly he was in an industry he didn't understand. Most people would have panicked or quit. Instead, he created a detailed six-month learning plan: specific algorithms to master, portfolio projects to build, companies to target, interview preparation timeline. He allocated specific hours each week. Within six months, he had three strong job offers and moved to an AI team at a leading company.

What makes him genuinely good at planning is that he's self-aware enough to know he needed a plan, and disciplined enough to follow it even when distractions appeared. His planning has influenced how I think about my own life. I've seen how having a real plan transforms possibility into reality.

**P3**

**Q: Do you think it's important to plan ahead?**

Absolutely. Planning creates clarity and increases your chances of achieving goals. Without a plan, you just react daily. With a plan, you're proactive. Planning forces you to think through obstacles before you hit them, so you fail less often. The downside is plans can get rigid—life doesn't follow plans. So I think the best approach is flexible planning: clear about your destination but willing to adjust the route.

**Q: What activities do we need to plan ahead?**

Big life decisions definitely—career changes, education, major purchases, family planning. Long-term projects like writing a book need planning because you break them into steps. Travel planning prevents wasted time and money. Financial planning is critical for retirement and emergencies. Even personal relationships benefit from planning: date nights, family time, important conversations need scheduling or they disappear.

**Q: Do you think children should plan their future careers?**

To some extent, yes, but not rigidly. Children are learning about themselves and the world, so detailed career planning at twelve might backfire. But it's healthy for teenagers to think about their interests and what work appeals to them. Not committing to one career, just exploring and understanding that choices matter. Teaching planning skills—breaking goals into steps, allocating time—matters regardless of their eventual career.

**Q: Should children ask teachers or parents for advice when making plans?**

Both, in different ways. Teachers understand education systems and how skills connect to careers. Parents understand the child's personality, values, family circumstances. Ideally, a child hears from both. Parents know their child loves building things, teachers suggest engineering. The combination is more valuable. But ultimately, children make their own decisions. Advice should inform, not determine, plans.

**Q: Is making study plans popular among young people?**

Not as much as it should be. Many study reactively—night before exams—rather than systematically. Some use study apps, but often superficially. The most successful students plan: knowing what topics to master, scheduling specific study times, scheduling reviews. Honestly, most young people don't do this until they fail. Students who plan from the beginning succeed more easily.

**Q: Do you think choosing a college major is closely related to future career?**

It's related but not deterministic. Engineering leads to engineering jobs, medicine to medical practice. But many successful people work in completely different fields. I studied computer science, worked in tech—direct path. But I know people who studied philosophy and became product managers. The major matters most for fields requiring certification. For others, it's how you develop skills and networks during college, not the official major title.

# 题7：喜欢画画的孩子 【儿子】

> **Describe a child who likes to draw.**
> You should say:
> - Who the child is
> - How you know the child
> - What the child draws
> - And explain how you feel about it

**P2**

The child I'm thinking of is my own son. He's five years old, and drawing has become his primary way of expressing himself. I notice his obsession because he's normally quite shy and reserved around strangers. Yet when he sits with paper and colored pencils, he transforms into someone focused, creative, completely absorbed.

His favorite subject is dinosaurs. He draws T-Rexes repeatedly with different scenarios—one showed a dinosaur piloting a spaceship, another eating ice cream for his birthday. He also draws LEGO creations with imaginative details.

Our refrigerator is covered with his artwork. The quality isn't technically refined, but what strikes me is the consistency and personal voice. He signs his name carefully at the bottom. When I ask him about a drawing, he explains the story in detail.

What I feel is genuine pride, but also something deeper: relief. My son struggles verbally with people he doesn't know well and gets anxious at kindergarten. But drawing gives him a voice when words fail. He's communicating through art something he can't articulate in conversation. I'm proud of his artistic ability, but I'm more grateful he's found a way to express himself authentically. I encourage his drawing without pushing and display his work prominently so he knows it matters.

**P3**

**Q: What is the right age for a child to learn drawing?**

Children naturally start drawing around two or three, and that's the right time to give them paper and crayons. Formal instruction—technique and perspective—is probably better at seven or eight when they have more patience. Younger children benefit from unstructured drawing without judgment. What's important is that adults don't pressure children to draw "correctly." At younger ages, the goal should be confidence and expression, not technical skill.

**Q: Why do most children draw more often than adults?**

Children have uninhibited creativity. They haven't learned to worry whether something is "good." Drawing is play, not performance. Adults become self-conscious, comparing themselves to professionals. Also, children have more free time. Adults are busy with work and responsibilities. Adults stop drawing because they judge themselves, and they judge themselves because education failed to teach them that drawing isn't about perfection—it's expression.

**Q: Why do some people visit galleries instead of viewing art online?**

Standing in front of original artwork is completely different than photos on a screen. The scale, texture, subtle colors that cameras miss—these create emotional impact that reproduction can't capture. There's something about being in a space with others looking at art together—shared cultural experience. Galleries are quiet, intentional spaces for contemplation. Online viewing is convenient but you swipe past without really seeing.

**Q: Should galleries and museums be free?**

I think they should be free or very affordable. Art and culture are our shared heritage, access shouldn't depend on wealth. Free admission lets more people, especially lower-income children, access cultural experiences. Museums need expensive curation and maintenance, so they need funding from government, donations, sponsorship. But entry barriers shouldn't exclude ordinary people. I believe broad cultural access outweighs revenue loss.

**Q: How do artworks inspire people?**

Artworks inspire by showing new perspectives or expressing feelings we couldn't articulate. A painting might inspire someone to pursue creativity. Art inspires by example—seeing what humans create motivates you to create. The best art inspires not just emotion but action. When my son sees other artists and creates his own, that's inspiration becoming generative. It's active motivation, not passive appreciation.

**Q: What are the differences between reading a book and visiting a museum?**

Reading is intimate and personal. You control pace, reread passages, you're alone with your thoughts. It's solitary, deeply focused. Museums are public, shared experiences. You move at your own pace in communal space. Reading needs sustained attention; museums invite you to stay with what captures you. Reading develops language through words; museums stimulate visual thinking. Reading is introspective, museums are about collective experience.

Zhang Wei introduced me to it about three years ago. When he showed me his setup—multiple databases, automated workflows—I realized how much more organized I could be.

Initially, I used Notion only for work: tracking project status, documenting technical decisions, consolidating meeting notes. But gradually I've expanded it to nearly every area of my life. I use it for reading lists with personal notes, family budgets, travel planning, goal tracking. My wife and I share a database for household projects and expenses.

**P3**

**Q: What apps or programs are popular among young people?**

Young people mainly use communication and social apps: WhatsApp, Douyin, WeChat, Instagram. These are for socializing and staying connected. They use Google Drive for collaborative work, though many reluctantly. Educational apps like Duolingo are popular, gaming apps obviously. But the most popular apps by far are social and entertainment—content consumption rather than creation.

**Q: Do older people and younger people use the same apps?**

No, significant generational divide. Older people use established apps: email, WeChat, Facebook. Younger people adopt new apps and use multiple platforms simultaneously. Older people find apps confusing that younger people find intuitive. Older people value stability; younger people are comfortable with continuously changing apps. **Q: Should children be limited in using apps?**

Yes, definitely, but thoughtfully not blanket. Young children shouldn't have unlimited access to addictive apps—social media algorithms are designed to hook users, children's brains especially vulnerable. Children should have restricted screen time, productivity apps more than entertainment. Complete prohibition doesn't work. Guidance is better: parents teaching kids to use apps as tools, not mindless time-sinks.

**Q: Do you think some countries should ban children from using social media?**

I understand the impulse. Social media is genuinely harmful for children: mental health problems, disrupted sleep, bullying, addictive design exploits adolescent psychology. Some countries like France and Australia implemented age restrictions. This is reasonable, especially under fourteen or fifteen. Science shows social media affects teenage brains negatively. But total bans are impractical. Better approach: strong regulation, removing addictive features, preventing algorithmic amplification of extreme content.

# 题9：微笑的场合 【成都熊猫基地】

> **Describe an occasion when everyone around you was smiling.**
> You should say:
> - When it happened
> - Where it happened
> - Who you were with
> - And explain why everyone was smiling

**P2**

It happened on a Saturday morning last spring at the Chengdu Panda Base. My wife and I took our son there — we arrived early, and there was already a crowd of maybe a hundred people, mostly families with kids.

They released a baby panda into this little play area with climbing structures. The thing kept trying to climb a tree, falling off, shaking itself, and going right back at it. Pretty adorable.

But what really got me was the crowd's reaction. By the third or fourth attempt, I looked around and realised everyone was smiling — like, nobody decided to smile, it just happened. Complete strangers, all watching this clumsy little panda, and you could feel this warm, shared amusement. No one was talking or taking photos — just watching and grinning.

My son, who's normally quite shy around strangers, was laughing harder than I'd ever seen. When the panda finally made it up, the whole crowd sort of exhaled together — this quiet collective satisfaction.

What struck me was how one silly little animal connected a hundred strangers. We weren't performing or trying to be friendly — we were all just sharing the same moment of pure joy, and honestly, that felt really special.

**P3**

**Q: Do you think people who like to smile are more friendly?**

I think smiling indicates warmth and openness, so yes, generally people who smile seem friendlier. Smiling is genuine expression and social signal. But constant smiling can be performative. The best smile is involuntary—like at the panda base. That authenticity reads as true friendliness. Someone smiling genuinely when amused is more trustworthy than someone doing it out of obligation.

**Q: Why do most people smile in photographs?**

Because smiling is the agreed-upon way to show pleasure in a frozen moment. A photo captures one moment, and a smile signals "I was happy." We want to look good in photographs, and smiling is the universal code. Young people smile genuinely in selfies now because they take hundreds. Older generations smile artificially in formal photos because that was convention when they were young.

**Q: Do women smile more than men? Why?**

Research suggests women do smile more, though it's cultural rather than biological. Women are socialized to be pleasant and accommodating, so smiling becomes learned behavior. Men are socialized to be stoic, so they smile less professionally. But this is changing. Younger men smile more naturally, younger women feel less pressure. Smiling patterns reflect gender norms, not fundamentals. As expectations shift, patterns will too.

**Q: Do people smile more when younger or older?**

Young children smile naturally and frequently without self-consciousness. As people age and experience disappointment, smiling often decreases. Middle-aged people—forties and fifties—might smile differently than teenagers. Their smiles might be less frequent but deeper. Older people often have a calm, contented smile. Teenagers smile often but sometimes nervously or performatively. Children smile most frequently overall.

**Q: Is smiling important in your culture?**

In Chinese culture, smiling is valued as respect and approachability, especially professionally. We emphasize harmony and not showing negative emotions publicly. Chinese people sometimes smile more performatively than express genuine emotion. There's a concept of maintaining face, and smiling is part of that. > You should say:
> - When it was
> - Who this person is
> - Why he/she apologized to you
> - And how you felt about it

**P2**

This happened about six months ago at home. My wife was doing a major clean-up of our apartment and accidentally threw away a box of old books I'd been keeping since university. Among them was my original copy of a computer science textbook that had personal notes in every margin—years of annotations I could never recreate.

When I came home and noticed the books were gone, I was genuinely upset. These weren't just books; they were a record of how I'd learned to think about problems over a decade. I didn't shout, but my wife could see how disappointed I was.

She apologized immediately and sincerely. She said, "I'm really sorry. I thought they were old textbooks you'd never open again. I should have asked you first." What touched me was that she didn't make excuses or get defensive. She acknowledged the mistake clearly and took full responsibility.

Over the next few days, she actually went online and tracked down the same edition of the textbook—not easy since it was out of print. She found a second-hand copy and gave it to me with a note saying, "I know the annotations are gone, but maybe you can start fresh."

**P3**

**Q: Should people apologize for anything wrong they do?**

I believe yes, if the wrongdoing affected others. An apology acknowledges harm and shows respect for the other person. But the apology should be genuine—meaningless apologies can actually damage trust more than no apology at all. Apologies are most valuable when paired with action, like my wife going out of her way to find a replacement for what she'd thrown away.

**Q: Do people in your country like to say "sorry"?**

Chinese culture is somewhat indirect about apologies, I'd say. Younger people, particularly those educated internationally, adopt more direct apology practices. Older generations tend to apologize more obliquely—through actions or implicit acknowledgment rather than direct verbal apologies. But I'm observing this changing. In professional contexts, particularly in international or tech companies, direct apologies have become normal. In family contexts, apologies are still sometimes implicit rather than explicitly stated.

**Q: On what occasions do people usually apologize?**

People typically apologize after causing harm—injuring someone physically, damaging property, or hurting feelings. Professional contexts generate apologies around missed deadlines, errors in work, or public criticism. Relationships involve apologies around broken promises, betrayal of trust, or hurtful comments. Family settings often involve apologies between partners or parents apologizing to children for losing patience. Social situations involve apologies for interrupting, forgetting important events, or violating social norms.

**Q: Why do some people refuse to say "sorry"?**

Some people view apologies as weakness or loss of status, especially in competitive or hierarchical environments. Others were raised in cultures or families where apologies were rare and learned that denying fault was the expected response. Some people struggle with ego—admitting error feels threatening to their self-image. There's also the complication of legal contexts where apologies can be interpreted as admission of liability.

**Q: Do you think every "sorry" is from the bottom of the heart?**

Definitely not. Many apologies are performative—said to end an uncomfortable situation rather than from genuine remorse. You can usually tell the difference: a sincere apology is specific about what went wrong and leads to real change in behavior. My wife's apology felt sincere precisely because she followed it with action—finding that replacement book.

**Q: Are women better than men at recognizing emotions?**

Research suggests women tend to be slightly better at identifying and articulating emotions on average, though there's enormous individual variation. This might partly reflect socialization—girls are generally encouraged to discuss feelings more than boys. But I'm cautious about overgeneralizing. I know men who are extraordinarily emotionally intelligent and women who are emotionally avoidant. Emotional recognition also depends on intelligence type, upbringing, and individual personality.

# 题37：学习朋友好习惯 【张伟读书习惯】

**P2**

I'd like to tell you about my friend Zhang Wei and a habit of his that I genuinely want to develop for myself. Zhang Wei has this consistent morning reading habit—he dedicates thirty minutes every morning to reading before he starts work, no matter how busy his schedule is.

I first noticed this habit when I stayed at his apartment during a business trip. I woke up relatively early, maybe around seven o'clock, expecting to be the first awake. But I found Zhang Wei already sitting in his living room with a cup of tea, deeply absorbed in a book. When I asked him about it, he explained that this thirty-minute morning reading ritual is non-negotiable in his routine. He reads various things—technical books related to his work, philosophy, biographies, contemporary essays. The content varies, but the habit is consistent.

What impressed me was his philosophical reasoning. He said that starting the day with focused reading gives his mind a calm, intentional beginning. **Q: What habits should children have?**

Children should develop habits that promote health, learning, and character. Physical activity habits—playing outdoors, sports, basic exercise—establish healthy baselines they'll ideally maintain. Reading habits expose children to language and imagination early. Good hygiene and sleep habits establish foundations for lifelong health. I think intellectual curiosity habits matter tremendously—approaching questions with thoughtfulness rather than quick judgments.

**Q: What should parents do to teach children good habits?**

Parents primarily teach through modeling—children internalize what they consistently observe. I notice my son imitating behaviors I didn't deliberately try to teach simply because he sees me doing them. Beyond modeling, parents should make good habits convenient and attractive. If a parent wants a child to read, having books visible and accessible and reading together makes it appealing.

**Q: What influences do children with bad habits have on other children?**

Unfortunately, bad habits can spread through peer influence quite readily. If one child in a classroom is disruptive, it often influences others to behave similarly—disruption becomes normalized. Similarly, if a child with an unkind habit of excluding others demonstrates this repeatedly, other children sometimes imitate it. Academic habits also spread—if one child avoids homework and faces minimal consequences, others notice.

**Q: Why do some habits change when people get older?**

Habits often change because circumstances change—you physically can't maintain the same habits when your life structure shifts. Someone athletic in school might stop exercising when they have demanding work, then restart exercise when they retire and have time. Additionally, habits connected to identity sometimes change as identity evolves.

**Q: How do we develop bad habits?**

Usually gradually and without intention. A bad habit typically starts as a one-time choice or response to a specific situation. But if that choice becomes repeated—often because it provides immediate relief or reward—it gradually establishes itself. Stress frequently triggers bad habit formation; people eat excessively, drink, or engage in avoidance behaviors when stressed.

**Q: What can we do to get rid of bad habits?**

The first step is genuine recognition and acceptance that you have the habit—denial prevents change. Next, identify the trigger or underlying need; if you eat when stressed, stress is the real issue that needs addressing. Replace the bad habit with a positive one that meets the same need—if you stress-eat, perhaps exercise or meditation addresses the stress more healthily.

Dangerous activities teach people about their own capabilities and limits. You discover what you're actually capable of under pressure versus what you merely assume. If you do something genuinely scary and survive it, your sense of what's possible expands.

**Q: What are the benefits of trying new things?**

Personal growth is fundamental. Trying new things breaks you out of autopilot existence and forces you to engage actively with your life. You discover capacities you didn't know you had and sometimes realize past limitations were self-imposed. New experiences create memories and perspective—cycling through Kyoto created different memories than a bus tour would have. They combat stagnation; many people feel stuck in life because they're not regularly challenging themselves.
