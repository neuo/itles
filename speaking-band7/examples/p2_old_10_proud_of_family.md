## P3 follow-ups（答案）

**Q1. When would parents feel proud of their children?**
I'd say parents feel proudest when their kids show good character, not just good marks. For instance, when a child's kind to others or owns up to a mistake, that's the real win. Honestly, a top exam score is nice, but seeing your kid grow into a decent, caring person means far more in the long run.

**Q2. Should parents reward children? Why and how?**
Definitely, within reason. A small reward tells a kid their effort gets noticed, and that keeps them motivated. But I'd steer clear of cash or pricey toys, since that turns everything into a deal. Instead, I reckon praise, a fun outing, or extra playtime works better — it feels warm rather than like a transaction.

**Q3. Is it good to reward children too often? Why?**
No, I don't reckon so. If you reward a kid for every little task, they start expecting a prize before they'll lift a finger. To be fair, it kills the joy of doing something just because it's right. So I'd save rewards for genuine effort, otherwise they lose all their meaning pretty fast.

**Q4. On what occasions would adults be proud of themselves?**
Honestly, adults feel proudest after pushing through something tough — like finishing a marathon or nailing a project they nearly gave up on. It's that sense of "I actually did it" that sticks. Plus, helping someone else can spark real pride too, since you realise you've made a small difference in their day.

**Q5. Do rewards help a child become better?**
To some extent, yes. A timely reward can nudge a child to try harder and build a good habit early on. But it's a bit of a double-edged sword — lean on it too much, and they only work for the prize. So I'd say it helps best as a gentle boost, not the main driver.

**Q6. What do you think about children working hard just for grades?**
Honestly, I find it a bit sad. Grades matter, sure, but if a kid only studies to chase numbers, they miss the joy of actually learning something. To be fair, the pressure's often from parents or schools. I reckon real curiosity lasts far longer than any report card ever could.
