## P3 follow-ups（答案）

**Q1. What can children do to help their parents?**
Quite a lot, honestly. Younger kids can tidy their rooms or set the table, while teenagers can cook simple meals or run errands. I'd say even small gestures count, because they show parents the effort is genuine. For instance, just remembering to take the bins out without being asked can really lighten a parent's load.

**Q2. Should children help their parents with household chores?**
Absolutely, they should. I reckon chores teach kids responsibility and stop them assuming meals and clean clothes just appear by magic. It's also a fair way to share the load, since one parent shouldn't carry everything. For example, a child who learns to wash up early on grows into a far more capable adult later.

**Q3. What kind of help do people need when looking for a new job?**
Quite a few kinds, really. They often need solid advice on writing a CV or handling interviews, since those skills get rusty fast. They also need encouragement, because rejection knocks your confidence. To be fair, a personal referral matters most — someone vouching for you can open a door that a polished application alone never would.

**Q4. Who should people ask for help, colleagues or family members?**
It really depends on the problem. For practical, work-related issues, I'd ask colleagues, since they understand the context and can offer hands-on tips. But for emotional support or big life decisions, family wins, because they know you deeply and won't judge you. So honestly, the smart move is matching the helper to what you actually need.

**Q5. Do you think schools should teach children to do household chores?**
I think they should, to some extent. Schools already teach cooking or basic life skills in some places, and that's brilliant, since not every family bothers to teach them. It builds independence too. Having said that, the main responsibility still sits with parents at home, so schools should just reinforce what's started there.

**Q6. Why are employees reluctant to ask their managers for help?**
Mostly it's pride and fear, I'd say. People worry that asking makes them look incompetent, so they'd rather struggle quietly than risk that impression. There's also the power gap, since managers control promotions and reviews. To be fair, a supportive boss who admits their own mistakes can really break down that wall over time.
