## P3 follow-ups（答案）

**Q1. Why should children have ambitions?**
I'd say it gives them something to aim for, which keeps them motivated when school gets dull. A kid who dreams of being a vet, for instance, will happily slog through biology. Without any goal, it's easy to drift and just coast. So ambitions give young people a bit of direction and purpose early on.

**Q2. What do you think of people going after high positions?**
Honestly, I'm fine with it as long as they're not stepping on others to get there. Wanting a top job often shows drive, and someone with that hunger usually pushes a whole team forward. But if it tips into pure ego, where titles matter more than the actual work, then it starts to feel a bit hollow.

**Q3. Why are some young people keen on being fans of superstars?**
Well, I reckon stars give them someone to look up to and dream about. A teenager who idolises a singer often picks up that person's confidence and style, almost like a role model. Plus, being a fan is social — you bond with other fans, swap stories, feel part of something. It's a sense of belonging, really.

**Q4. Is it good for a person to be ambitious?**
Mostly, yes. Being ambitious pushes you to grow and not settle for less than you could be. Someone with goals tends to wake up with a clearer sense of why they're doing things. Having said that, it can backfire if you're never satisfied, so it works best when it's balanced with knowing when enough is enough.

**Q5. Do you think it is necessary to be ambitious when working in a team in a company?**
Not for everyone, no. A team needs steady players too, people who just do solid work without chasing the spotlight. If everybody's fiercely ambitious, you'd get clashing egos and constant friction. So I'd say one or two driven members help, but the rest can simply be reliable and cooperative. Balance matters more than blanket ambition.

**Q6. Should parents support their children in pursuing their ambitions?**
Absolutely, within reason. A bit of backing — encouragement, maybe some lessons — can turn a vague dream into something real. Without it, kids often give up at the first hurdle. But parents shouldn't push their own unfulfilled goals onto them. The trick is supporting whatever the child genuinely wants, not what the parent secretly wishes they'd chosen.
